## The passive listener: why GPS satellites do not track you or receive signals from your phone
How can your smartphone pinpoint your exact location anywhere on Earth just by receiving silent radio signals? In reality, the Global Positioning System is a completely one-way passive broadcast network. GPS operates independently of telephone or Internet reception. Owned by the United States Space Force, the satellite system is freely accessible to anyone. The constellation consists of at least 24 active satellites arranged across six distinct orbital planes. The first prototype spacecraft went into orbit in 1978 before reaching full operational status in 1995. Each satellite completes two full orbits around Earth during every sidereal day. As they orbit, these satellites continuously broadcast radio signals containing their exact position and precise time. Your phone acts as a silent receiver, quietly catching these incoming radio transmissions from the sky.
## Turning time into space: how measuring radio travel times reveals your distance from an orbit
To figure out your exact location, your device converts time measurements directly into distance measurements. GPS satellites circle the Earth in medium orbit at an altitude of approximately 20,200 kilometres. Radio signals broadcast from these satellites travel through space at the constant speed of light. Crucially, the speed of radio waves is constant and completely independent of the satellite's speed. Each satellite continually broadcasts a pseudorandom sequence of ones and zeros known to the receiver. The transmission message includes a timestamp recording the exact time the signal left the satellite. When your device receives the signal, it records the arrival time using its own internal clock. By subtracting the transmission time from the arrival time, the receiver calculates the signal delay. Multiplying this time value by the speed of light yields a pseudorange rather than an exact distance. Because light moves so fast, a tiny timing offset of just 10 nanoseconds causes a 3 metre distance error.
## The sphere intersection: using trilateration to narrow your place on Earth to two points
A single distance measurement places your device somewhere on a giant spherical shell centred on that satellite. To narrow down your location, your phone needs signals from additional satellites orbiting above. When you receive a distance measurement from a second satellite, a second sphere is created. Taking several distance measurements allows a receiver to find the point where the spherical shells meet. Your receiver generates a position fix by finding where multiple spherical shells meet. Adding a third satellite creates a third spherical surface around that third broadcasting source. Combining multiple distance measurements allows the receiver to determine the point where the shells meet. In special cases where elevation is already known, three satellites can determine a position. This geometric technique of calculating positions using measured distances is known as trilateration.
## The fourth satellite trick: how an extra signal fixes the cheap quartz clock in your pocket
Trilateration with three spheres assumes that your receiver clock is perfectly synchronized with the satellites. GPS satellites carry extremely stable atomic clocks that are monitored and synchronized regularly by ground stations. Because your phone clock is inaccurate, measured travel times produce biased distances called pseudoranges. To solve this problem without needing an impractical receiver clock, the system uses signals from a fourth satellite. Your GPS receiver requires signals from at least four satellites to solve its navigation equations. With four satellite signals, your phone calculates four unknown mathematical quantities simultaneously. These unknowns are three spatial coordinates representing position and the exact deviation of your phone clock. Solving the navigation equations gives receiver position and clock deviation, providing cheap and highly accurate timing. This clever mathematical approach keeps consumer receivers light, affordable, and practical for daily use.
## Einstein in orbit: why satellite clocks tick faster and how engineers correct for relativity
Even with four satellite signals, GPS would fail quickly without accounting for Albert Einstein's theories of relativity. Special relativity predicts that time passes slower for objects moving at higher speeds relative to an observer. Because satellites travel at roughly 3,874 metres per second, their atomic clocks tick slower. This speed effect causes orbital clocks to lose about 7.2 microseconds per day compared to ground clocks. Meanwhile, general relativity shows that gravity slows down time closer to massive bodies like Earth. Because satellites operate high above Earth's surface, they experience weaker gravity than receivers on the ground. This weaker gravity causes satellite clocks to tick faster by about 45.8 microseconds per day. Combining both relativistic effects, satellite clocks run faster by approximately 38.6 microseconds every day. If uncorrected, this timing difference would cause location errors of roughly 11.4 kilometres per day. To correct this, engineers pre-adjust the atomic clock frequencies before launching the satellites into space. They slow the clock rate from 10.23 megahertz down to 10.22999999543 megahertz. This pre-launch adjustment allows orbital clocks to stay synchronized with time on Earth's surface.
## Mapping the numbers: how raw coordinates turn into a moving blue dot on your screen
Once your device processes the satellite signals, it calculates raw three-dimensional position coordinates. The receiver then converts these spatial numbers into standard latitude, longitude, and height. Consumer devices like smartphones can achieve horizontal positioning accuracy of 4.9 metres or better. When paired with assistive services like Wi-Fi positioning, positioning performance becomes even more reliable. High-end applications like land surveying can even achieve sub-millimetre accuracy with long-term measurements. These calculated coordinates may be displayed on a moving map display or used by another system. An internal tracker algorithm combines successive measurements to predict movement and filter out random errors. By analyzing signal Doppler frequency shifts, your device can also calculate your exact velocity and direction. More advanced navigation systems combine these satellite data with internal compasses or motion sensors. Your phone finds your exact location by listening to broadcast timestamps, measuring radio travel times, and calculating geometry.
