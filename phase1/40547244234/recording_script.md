- One file per scene, named s01, s02, ... (any phone format: m4a, mp3, wav). One file for the whole video also works.

## s01 — Part 1: Cosmic Beauty and Invisible Danger

1. But how close would an exploding star need to be to completely strip away Earth's protective atmosphere?
2. At peak optical brightness, a single supernova can be comparable to an entire galaxy.
3. Within our Milky Way galaxy, these violent stellar events occur roughly 1.6 to 4.6 times every century on average.
4. Billions of years ago, ancient supernovae formed heavy elements that enabled the chemical processes for life on Earth.

## s02 — Part 2: The First Wave of High-Energy Radiation

1. What would happen if a massive star exploded only a few dozen light-years away from our solar system?
2. Earth's atmosphere is remarkably effective at absorbing high-energy electromagnetic radiation like X-rays and gamma rays.
3. Surviving that initial burst of light might make it seem like our planet escaped the catastrophe completely unharmed.
4. However, the real destruction is caused by complex chemical reactions initiated in the upper layers of our air.
5. High above us, intense energy from incoming gamma rays begins breaking apart stable gas molecules in the upper atmosphere.

## s03 — Part 3: Chemical Destruction of the Ozone Shield

1. Incoming gamma radiation causes radiolysis, breaking apart standard diatomic nitrogen and oxygen molecules high in the atmosphere.
2. This reaction recombines molecular nitrogen and oxygen into dangerous gaseous compounds known as nitrogen oxides.
3. Nitrogen oxides deplete protective stratospheric ozone molecules.
4. Computer models show that a nearby explosion could cause a global ozone reduction of 25 to 35 per cent.
5. In specific regional locations of the upper atmosphere, local ozone loss could reach up to 75 per cent for years.
6. Depleting this shield exposes Earth's surface to bio-harmful solar ultraviolet radiation, causing widespread biological damage.

## s04 — Part 4: The Second Wave from Cosmic Rays

1. Supernovae expel several solar masses of stellar material into space at speeds reaching several per cent of light speed.
2. In the case of a Betelgeuse supernova, the stream of charged particles would reach Earth about 100,000 years after the initial light.

## s05 — Part 5: Calculating the Safe Threshold Distance

1. How close would a dying star actually need to be to permanently destroy Earth's protective atmospheric shield?
2. Scientists calculate dangerous stellar distances using atmospheric computer models and measured radiation flux from Supernova 1987A.
3. Fortunately, a supernova explosion occurring within 33 light-years of Earth is estimated to happen only once every 240 million years.
4. If a highly focused gamma-ray burst pointed directly at Earth, its focused beam could vapourise anything within 200 light-years.
5. In fact, a nearby gamma-ray burst may have triggered the Late Ordovician mass extinction on Earth.
6. That ancient cosmic event resulted in the death of nearly 60 per cent of oceanic life on Earth.

## s06 — Part 6: Mapping Our Stellar Neighbourhood

1. Are there any dangerous candidate stars close enough to threaten Earth with an atmospheric collapse today?
2. The famous red supergiant star Betelgeuse sits at a distance of roughly 400 to 600 light-years from our Sun.
3. When Betelgeuse detonates as a brilliant supernova within the next 100,000 years, it will shine as bright as a half-Moon.
4. Because Betelgeuse is hundreds of light-years away, life on Earth will be completely unharmed by its eventual explosion.
5. The closest known candidate for a white dwarf supernova is IK Pegasi, but it is moving away to a safe distance.
6. Even if other famous stars a few hundred light-years away explode in 1,000 years, Earth is in little danger.
7. As you gaze up at the night sky, how does it feel knowing our atmosphere keeps us safe from distant cosmic explosions?
