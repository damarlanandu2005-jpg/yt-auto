## s01 — The Paradox of Time and Distance in the Universe

1. How can the observable universe be 93 billion light-years across if it is only 13.8 billion years old?
2. If light travels at a constant speed, this vast cosmic scale sounds like an impossible contradiction.
3. So why does this cosmic puzzle matter so much when we look out into space?
4. Objects beyond this physical boundary cannot be detected yet because their light has not had enough time to reach Earth.
5. In this video, we will answer three fundamental questions about our cosmic horizon in plain words.
6. How does space stretch while light is actively traveling across the universe?
7. How does a travel time of 13.8 billion years convert into a physical radius of 46.5 billion light-years?
8. And what mysterious regions lie forever beyond the edge of our current observable cosmos?
9. To solve this mystery, we must reframe how we imagine cosmic space and time.

## s02 — The False Picture of Galaxies Flying Through Empty Space

1. Many people visualize the universe as a huge empty room where galaxies rocket through a dark void.
2. In that false mental picture, galaxies are racing through a fixed, static space like cosmic shrapnel.
3. If that picture were correct, nothing could ever appear farther away than 13.8 billion light-years.
4. But this common picture of cosmic expansion is completely wrong.
5. Cosmic expansion is not galaxies moving through a pre-existing empty space.
6. Instead, general relativity describes expansion as an intrinsic increase in distance between cosmic objects over time.
7. Most galaxies are nearly at rest relative to their local comoving spatial coordinates.
8. The velocity of a galaxy relative to its local comoving grid is called its peculiar velocity.
9. These local peculiar velocities are caused mostly by gravity and are usually quite small.
10. So how should we picture the movement of galaxies as the universe expands?
11. Understanding this key distinction changes everything about how we calculate cosmic distances.

## s03 — Space as an Expanding Fabric Rather Than a Static Box

1. To understand cosmic distances, imagine space as an expanding fabric rather than a rigid stage.
2. Mathematically, cosmologists track this growth using a value called the cosmic scale factor.
3. The scale factor grows over time, describing how physical distances expand across the universe.
4. In the early history of the universe, the scale factor was extremely small.
5. That means all matter in the young cosmos was squeezed much closer together.
6. Before recombination, the universe was filled with an opaque white-hot fog of plasma.
7. Once neutral atoms formed, ancient photons became free to travel freely through transparent space.
8. These released photons created the cosmic microwave background radiation that fills space today.
9. When this ancient light was first emitted, its source was only 42 million light-years from Earth.
10. Over 13.8 billion years, continuous expansion stretched that distance dramatically.

## s04 — Light Traveling Through Expanding Space

1. How does a light wave move through space that expands while the light is moving?
2. Imagine an ant crawling along a stretching rubber band toward your hand.
3. The ant crawls forward at a steady pace relative to the rubber directly under its feet.
4. Similarly, a photon always travels through space locally at the exact speed of light.
5. A local comoving observer will always measure the speed of light as the standard constant speed.
6. But while the photon travels forward, the space behind it continues to expand over time.
7. At the same time, the space ahead of the photon stretches as well.
8. Because space stretches everywhere, the distance between the photon and its source grows continuously.
9. Photons from the cosmic microwave background spent 13.8 billion years traveling across space to reach Earth.
10. But during those 13.8 billion years, expansion pushed the matter that emitted those photons much farther away.

## s05 — How 13.8 Billion Light-Years Turns Into a 46.5 Billion Light-Year Radius

1. So, how far away is that emitting matter located at the present cosmological time?
2. To understand this, we must distinguish between light-travel distance and proper distance.
3. Light-travel distance is simply the age of the universe multiplied by the speed of light.
4. That calculation yields a light-travel distance of about 13.8 billion light-years.
5. Proper distance represents the actual physical distance between two objects at a single moment in time.
6. You can imagine proper distance as the value measured by rulers if expansion suddenly froze.
7. Comoving distance factors out expansion and equals proper distance at the present age of the universe.
8. From Earth, the comoving distance to the edge of our observable sphere is about 46.5 billion light-years.
9. This radial distance marks our particle horizon, which limits what signals can reach us.
10. Thus, our observable universe is a sphere with a radius of 46.5 billion light-years.

## s06 — Measuring the 93 Billion Light-Year Horizon

1. Because Earth receives light from all directions, our observable horizon forms a massive sphere.
2. Every point in the universe has its own distinct observable sphere centered on its location.
3. To find the diameter of Earth's observable sphere, we double the 46.5 billion light-year radius.
4. Doubling that radius yields a full cosmic diameter of 93 billion light-years across.
5. That total count means there are more stars in our view than beach sand grains on Earth.
6. This ordinary matter includes stars, planets, interstellar gas, and intergalactic gas, but excludes dark matter.
7. Every object within this sphere is visible because its light had time to travel to Earth.

## s07 — What Lies Beyond Our Cosmic Horizon

1. So, how can the observable universe be 93 billion light-years wide after only 13.8 billion years?
2. Space itself stretched continuously while light made its long journey across the expanding universe.
3. That cosmic expansion turned 13.8 billion years of light travel into a 46.5 billion light-year physical radius.
4. Doubling that radius gives the full observable diameter of 93 billion light-years today.
5. Objects located beyond our particle horizon have emitted light that has not yet reached Earth.
6. The unobservable portion of the entire universe may be far larger, or even infinite in size.
7. Furthermore, dark energy is currently driving an accelerated expansion of space.
8. Because expansion accelerates, astronomers calculate a future visibility limit at 62 billion light-years comoving distance.
9. Light emitted today by objects farther than 16 billion light-years will never reach us in the infinite future.
10. Distant galaxies outside our local supercluster will eventually appear to freeze, turn redder, and fade away.
11. Take a moment to think about how much of our universe will remain forever beyond your reach.
